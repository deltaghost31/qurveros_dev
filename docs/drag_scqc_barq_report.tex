\title{Incorporating DRAG into Space-Curve Quantum Control\\
and Leakage-Aware BARQ}
\author{Technical design note for the \texttt{qurveros} package}
\date{23 October 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
This note derives how Derivative Removal by Adiabatic Gate (DRAG) can be
combined with space-curve quantum control (SCQC) and the B\'ezier ansatz for
robust quantum control (BARQ), as implemented in \texttt{qurveros}.  The
central result is that a DRAG correction applied after a completed BARQ design
is inexpensive, but it does not automatically preserve the exact geometric
robustness conditions.  It preserves the computational gate only
perturbatively in \(1/(\alpha T)\) and generally introduces mixed errors of
order \((\delta_z T)^2/(\alpha T)^2\).  A leakage-aware BARQ objective can
instead impose four real first-order leakage constraints while recomputing
dephasing and amplitude sensitivities under the corrected qutrit evolution.
The note also records the associated boundary conditions, objective functions,
spectral and smoothness extensions, the relation to a perturbative
\(\mathbb{C}^2\) leakage curve, and the scaling that can reasonably be
expected before a numerical campaign.
\end{abstract}

\section{Scope}

This note keeps the existing single-qubit SCQC and BARQ constructions and asks
what changes when the same pulses drive a weakly anharmonic three-level
system.  DRAG is a pulse correction for that setting~\cite{Motzoi2009}.  It is
applied after the curve has been converted to fields.  Sections~\ref{sec:scqc}
and~\ref{sec:barq-review} summarize the geometric formalism first.
Section~\ref{sec:notation} fixes every symbol used later and records where it
differs from \texttt{qurveros}, from Motzoi~et~al., and from the SCQC papers.
Only then does the note introduce leakage and DRAG.

\section{Single-qubit SCQC, with and without BARQ}
\label{sec:scqc}

\subsection{Single-qubit SCQC without BARQ}
\label{sec:scqc-single}

The single-qubit drive used by Barnes~et~al.~\cite{BarnesReview}, including
quasistatic dephasing, is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:hc-qubit}
\begin{aligned}
  \frac{H(t)}{\hbar}
  &=\frac{\Omega(t)}{2}\bigl(\cos\Phi(t)\,\sigma_x+\sin\Phi(t)\,\sigma_y\bigr)\\
  &\quad+\frac{\Delta_c(t)+\delta_z}{2}\sigma_z.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here \(\sigma_x,\sigma_y,\sigma_z\) are the Pauli operators on
\(\{\ket0,\ket1\}\), \(\Omega(t)\) is the Rabi frequency, \(\Phi(t)\) is the
drive phase, and \(\Delta_c(t)\) is the control detuning from the qubit
transition.  The coefficient \(\delta_z\) is the quasistatic noise.  It is
constant during one gate but unknown, and it has the same units as
\(\Delta_c\).
Barnes~et~al.\ write the noise operator as \(\delta H=\sigma_z\) and carry its
small coefficient implicitly~\cite{BarnesReview}.  The factor \(\tfrac12\) in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:hc-qubit} matches the \texttt{qurveros} convention
\(H_n=\delta_z\sigma_z/2\).  Because this factor multiplies the whole error
integral, it does not change the closure or vanishing-area conditions.  In the
package, \(\delta_z\) is the additive shift used by
\texttt{static\_dephasing\_experiment}.

Let \(U_c(t)\) be the evolution generated by the first three terms only, with
\(\ii\hbar\dot U_c=H_cU_c\) and \(U_c(0)=I\).  The first-order error is the
curve
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:error-curve}
  \int_0^t U_c^\dagger(t')\sigma_z U_c(t')\,\dd t'
  =\mathbf{r}(t)\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma}
  =x(t)\sigma_x+y(t)\sigma_y+z(t)\sigma_z.
\end{equation}
This is Eq.~(7) of the review~\cite{BarnesReview}.  The vector
\(\mathbf{r}(t)=(x,y,z)\) is an ordinary curve in three-dimensional Euclidean
space.  Its curvature \(\kappa\) and torsion \(\tau_c\) determine the controls:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kappa-tau}
  \kappa(t)=\Omega(t),
  \qquad
  \tau_c(t)=\dot\Phi(t)-\Delta_c(t).
\end{equation}
Thus a curve fixes the envelope and only the combination
\(\dot\Phi-\Delta_c\).  A frame rotation about \(z\) can move the torsion into
either the phase or the detuning.  That is why one curve has several control
modes.  In \texttt{qurveros} they are selected by \texttt{control\_mode}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{XY}: resonant drive, \(\Delta_c=0\), and \(\Phi\) is the
        integral of the torsion;
  \item \texttt{XZ}: \(\Phi=0\) and \(\Delta_c=-\tau_c\);
  \item \texttt{TTC}: a constant detuning compensates the total torsion, and
        the phase absorbs \(\Delta_c t\);
  \item \texttt{resTTC}: the same phase as \texttt{TTC}, but the detuning is
        then set back to zero.
\end{itemize}
The package also multiplies \(\Omega\) by a sign that flips at singular
inflection points, so the stored quadratures stay continuous when the Frenet
binormal reverses.  The curvature in \texttt{frenet\_dict} is the geometric
\(\kappa\), before that sign and before multiplication by the gate duration.

The gate implemented by the curve is read from the moving frame at the two
endpoints, up to the final phase gauge.  \texttt{qurveros} stores that gate as
the \(3\times3\) adjoint matrix \texttt{adj\_curve}.  No optimization is
required for this step: any sufficiently smooth curve, for example the circle
and Bessel examples, can be passed to \texttt{SpaceCurve}.

The robustness conditions used by the package are geometric constraints on
\(\mathbf{r}\).  With \(T\) the duration, equivalently the length of a
unit-speed curve:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Closure}, \(\mathbf{r}(T)=\mathbf{r}(0)\).  The first-order
        dephasing generator vanishes.  The code reports this, normalized by
        \(T\), as \texttt{closed\_test}.
  \item \emph{Vanishing projected area} of \(\mathbf{r}\).  This cancels the
        second-order dephasing term~\cite{BarnesReview}.  The code reports
        \texttt{curve\_area\_test}.
  \item \emph{Vanishing area of the tantrix}, the curve traced by the unit
        tangent.  This is the first-order condition for a multiplicative error
        in \(\Omega\).  The code reports \texttt{tantrix\_area\_test}.
  \item \emph{Curve flatness index}, the normalized energy
        \(\int\abs{\mathbf{r}}^2\,\dd s/T^3\).  It estimates infidelity under
        \(1/f^2\) noise and is reported as \texttt{CFI}.
\end{itemize}
Without BARQ these conditions are targets for whoever designs the curve.  They
are not built into an arbitrary \texttt{SpaceCurve}.

\subsection{Single-qubit SCQC with BARQ}
\label{sec:barq-review}

BARQ restricts the same three-dimensional curve to a B\'ezier parametrization.
In \texttt{qurveros} the corresponding object is \texttt{BarqCurve}.
The position is a B\'ezier curve on the
fixed interval \(s\in[0,1]\),
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  \mathbf{r}(s)&=\sum_{i=0}^{n} B_{i,n}(s)\,\mathbf{W}_i,\\
  B_{i,n}(s)&=\binom{n}{i}s^i(1-s)^{n-i}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
with control points \(\mathbf{W}_i\in\mathbb{R}^3\).  The Bernstein weights
\(B_{i,n}\) are not a drive amplitude.  Two structural choices are imposed
before any numerical search.

\emph{Closure and a vanishing endpoint envelope.}  The first and last control
points are set to the origin.  The next points are arranged so that the
curvature, and therefore \(\Omega\), vanishes at both endpoints.  In the code
this arrangement is \texttt{point\_gate\_fixing}.

\emph{Gate fixing.}  The same endpoint points are aligned with the adjoint
representation of the target unitary.  Consequently every later movement of the
free interior points preserves the ideal qubit gate and the closure condition,
to the accuracy of the two-level theory.  The free parameters are the interior
points, a dictionary of positive scale factors called the point-gate-fixing
parameters, and one angle \(\theta_B\).  The angle \(\theta_B\) is the
binormal angle at the final endpoint.  It is an input to the constant-detuning
calculation in \texttt{TTC} mode; it is not an anharmonicity and it is not the
DRAG coefficient introduced below.

An optional point-relation function can constrain the interior points further.
If it is omitted, those points pass through unchanged.  The numerical search
then minimizes a weighted sum of losses such as tantrix area, curve area, gate
duration, peak curvature, curve flatness, and the \texttt{TTC} detuning.  This
is ordinary single-qubit SCQC with part of the geometry fixed analytically.
\texttt{qurveros} does not implement a multi-qubit version of this
parametrization.

\subsection{Multi-qubit and multilevel SCQC}
\label{sec:scqc-multi}

The same error-curve idea extends beyond one qubit~\cite{Buterakos2021,BarnesReview},
but the curve is no longer three-dimensional and BARQ does not automatically
extend with it.  For a control Hamiltonian \(H_c\) and a noise Hamiltonian
\(\delta H\), the review defines the normalized noise direction by
\(Q_{\mathrm{B}}=\delta H/\abs{\delta H}\) and assumes
\(\{H_c,Q_{\mathrm{B}}\}=0\).  The subscript distinguishes this operator from
the leakage projector \(Q\) introduced in Section~\ref{sec:notation}.  Barnes
et~al.\ call the noise direction \(Q\).  The leading-order error is again an
integrated interaction-picture operator,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:multi-curve}
  \int_0^t U_c^\dagger(t')Q_{\mathrm{B}}U_c(t')\,\dd t'
  =\sum_{\alpha=1}^{d_E} x_\alpha(t)\,V_\alpha,
\end{equation}
which is their Eq.~(21).  The operators \(V_\alpha\) are the distinct
directions generated by conjugating \(Q_{\mathrm{B}}\), and \(d_E\) is the
dimension of the resulting Euclidean curve.  It is determined by the Lie
algebra generated by \(H_c\) and \(Q_{\mathrm{B}}\), not chosen freely.  A
single qubit with dephasing returns \(d_E=3\).  The two-qubit Ising example
worked out by Buterakos, Das~Sarma, and Barnes has \(d_E=6\), and in that
special case the curve splits into two coupled three-dimensional
curves~\cite{Buterakos2021}.

The controls are no longer a single curvature and torsion.  An orthonormal
Frenet--Serret frame \(\mathbf{e}_n(t)\) in \(d_E\) dimensions has generalized
curvatures \(\kappa_n\) obeying
\begin{equation}
  \dot{\mathbf{e}}_n=-\kappa_{n-1}\mathbf{e}_{n-1}+\kappa_n\mathbf{e}_{n+1},
\end{equation}
with \(\kappa_1\) the ordinary curvature and \(\kappa_2\) the ordinary torsion
when \(d_E=3\).  The review gives a recursion that identifies these curvatures
with nested commutators and time derivatives of \(H_c\).  Reading a pulse from
a curve is therefore still possible.  Designing the curve is harder: a
physical Hamiltonian often requires some \(\kappa_n\) to be constant or to be
functions of one another.  The review states that a general method for
imposing those constraints in dimension greater than three is open, and that
the single-qubit vanishing-area condition has not been extended to the
higher-dimensional curve.  Closure, \(\mathbf{r}(T)=\mathbf{r}(0)\), remains
the condition for cancelling the leading Magnus term.

Two consequences matter for the rest of this note.  First, multi-qubit SCQC
without BARQ already exists as this curvature construction, but the gate must
still be fixed by matching the endpoint frame, and that step is not automated
by \texttt{qurveros}.  Second, there is no BARQ-style B\'ezier gate-fixing
theorem in the package, or in the references above, for a generic multi-qubit
or qutrit curve.  Leakage in the review is motivation for leaving the Bloch
sphere.  It is not given a separate DRAG calculation.  The leakage conditions
derived below are perturbative additions to the single-qubit theory, not a
replacement for Eq.~\eqref{eq:multi-curve}.

\section{Notation and differences from the references}
\label{sec:notation}

Time \(t\) runs from \(0\) to the gate duration \(T\).  The normalized time used
throughout \texttt{qurveros} is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:s}
  s=\frac{t}{T}\in[0,1].
\end{equation}
A dot is \(\dd/\dd t\) and a prime is \(\dd/\dd s\).  The computational
projector and the leakage projector on a qutrit are
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:PQ}
  P=\ket0\bra0+\ket1\bra1,
  \qquad
  Q=I-P=\ket2\bra2
\end{equation}
in the three-level model.  For a larger truncation, \(Q\) is the projector onto
every state outside \(\{\ket0,\ket1\}\).  This \(Q\) is not the noise direction
\(Q_{\mathrm{B}}\) of Section~\ref{sec:scqc-multi}.

The driven three-level ladder, in the frame rotating at the carrier and after
the rotating-wave approximation, is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:h3}
\begin{aligned}
  \frac{H_3(t)}{\hbar}
  &=\delta(t)\ket1\bra1+\bigl(2\delta(t)+\alpha\bigr)\ket2\bra2 \\
  &\quad+\frac12\Bigl\{\eps(t)\bigl(\ket0\bra1+\lambda\ket1\bra2\bigr)
       +\text{h.c.}\Bigr\}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Each new symbol has a specific meaning:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \(\ket0,\ket1,\ket2\) are the lowest three eigenstates of the undriven
        anharmonic oscillator.
  \item \(\omega_{01}\) and \(\omega_{12}\) are their transition angular
        frequencies, in radians per unit time.  They are properties of the
        device, not pulse fields.
  \item \(\alpha=\omega_{12}-\omega_{01}\) is the anharmonicity.  For a
        transmon, \(\alpha<0\).  Motzoi~et~al.\ call this quantity \(\Delta\),
        with \(\Delta=\omega_2-2\omega_1\) in their lab-frame
        numbering~\cite{Motzoi2009}.  Barnes~et~al.\ use \(\Delta(t)\) for the
        control detuning.  This note never uses \(\Delta\) for the
        anharmonicity; the control detuning is \(\Delta_c\) in
        Eq.~\eqref{eq:hc-qubit} and \(\delta\) in Eq.~\eqref{eq:h3}.
  \item \(\delta(t)\) is the instantaneous detuning of the carrier below the
        \(\ket0\leftrightarrow\ket1\) transition, expressed as an angular
        frequency.  Motzoi~et~al.\ call the constant part of this detuning
        \(\delta_1\).  On the qubit subspace and at \(\lambda=0\),
        \(\delta(t)\) is exactly \(\Delta_c(t)\).
  \item \(\eps(t)=\Omega_x(t)+\ii\Omega_y(t)\) is the complex envelope.
        Matching Eq.~\eqref{eq:hc-qubit} requires
        \(\Omega_x=\Omega\cos\Phi\), \(\Omega_y=\Omega\sin\Phi\), and
        \(\Omega=\abs{\eps}\).  Motzoi~et~al.\ write the quadratures as
        \(\mathcal{E}^x\) and \(\mathcal{E}^y\).  Their \(\mathcal{E}\) is this
        \(\eps\), not the noise strength.
  \item \(\lambda\) is the ratio of the \(\ket1\leftrightarrow\ket2\) coupling
        to the \(\ket0\leftrightarrow\ket1\) coupling.  Motzoi~et~al.\ set
        \(\lambda_1=1\) and \(\lambda_2=\lambda\).  For a transmon,
        \(\lambda=\sqrt2\).  The SCQC references do not have this parameter
        because they use a two-level Hilbert space.
\end{itemize}
The sign of every DRAG quadrature below is tied to Eq.~\eqref{eq:h3}.
Reversing the sign in \(\eps=\Omega_x+\ii\Omega_y\), or replacing \(\alpha\) by
\(\abs\alpha\) while keeping the same formula, flips those signs.  It does not
change the geometric closure conditions.

The \texttt{qurveros} control dictionary does not store \(\eps(t)\) directly.
After \texttt{evaluate\_control\_dict}, \texttt{times} runs over \(s\in[0,1]\),
\texttt{omega} is a real signed envelope, \texttt{phi} is a phase, and
\texttt{delta} is a detuning.  Their relation to the physical fields is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:normalized}
\begin{aligned}
  u(s)&=T\eps(Ts)=\omega_{\mathrm{qv}}(s)\,e^{\ii\phi(s)},\\
  d(s)&=T\delta(Ts),\\
  \nu&=\alpha T,\\
  \eta&=\frac{1}{\abs\nu}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The four symbols on the left are the ones used in the leakage calculation:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \(u(s)\) is the dimensionless complex envelope.  Its real and imaginary
        parts are \(\omega_x(s)\) and \(\omega_y(s)\).
  \item \(\omega_{\mathrm{qv}}(s)\) is the number stored as
        \texttt{control\_dict['omega']}.  For a curve without inflection
        points it equals \(T\kappa(t)=T\Omega(t)\).  At an inflection it also
        includes the sign tracker described in Section~\ref{sec:scqc-single}.
        It is not the transition frequency \(\omega_{01}\).
  \item \(\phi(s)=\Phi(Ts)\) is \texttt{control\_dict['phi']}.
  \item \(d(s)\) is \texttt{control\_dict['delta']}.  Because the simulator
        integrates over \(s\in[0,1]\) and the stored \texttt{times} array
        already ends at \(1\), this number is the physical detuning multiplied
        by \(T\), not a second copy of that factor.
  \item \(\nu\) is the dimensionless anharmonicity.  It is the frequency at
        which the leakage integrals are evaluated.  It is not a curvature.
  \item \(\eta\) is the DRAG expansion parameter.  The expansion is appropriate
        when the envelope and phase vary slowly relative to \(\abs\alpha\), and
        it becomes less reliable when \(\abs{\alpha}T\) is only a few radians.
\end{itemize}

\begin{remark}[Three different statements about leakage]
\label{rem:three}
The derivations below keep three statements separate.  They use only symbols
already defined in this section.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Bare spectral notch.}  For an envelope \(\eps(t)\) that vanishes
        at both endpoints, the corrected envelope
        \(\eps-\ii\dot\eps/\alpha\) has zero Fourier component at angular
        frequency \(-\alpha\).  This statement does not involve \(U_c\) or the
        populations in the qutrit.
  \item \emph{First-order gate leakage.}  The amplitude to arrive in \(\ket2\)
        is the integral of \(u^*(s)e^{\ii\nu s}\) against the ideal qubit
        amplitude \(\braket{1}{\tilde U_q(s)j}\).  Setting those integrals to
        zero is stronger than a notch in \(u\) alone.
  \item \emph{Fixed-axis DRAG.}  Motzoi~et~al.\ remove the first leakage
        coupling by a frame change \(V(t)\) generated by the drive.  Their
        derivation assumes a real envelope \(\mathcal{E}^x(t)\) and a
        corrective quadrature \(\mathcal{E}^y(t)\).  The complex formula used
        for a general SCQC phase is the extension of that result to
        \(\eps(t)\), and it remains an ansatz until a qutrit calculation or a
        simulation checks it.  A Schrieffer--Wolff calculation means precisely
        this elimination of the off-resonant coupling by a near-identity
        unitary; no additional symbol is introduced by that name.
\end{enumerate}
\end{remark}

\section{First-order leakage}
\label{sec:leakage}

The amplitude \eqref{eq:dyson} below is first-order time-dependent perturbation
theory for the \(1\leftrightarrow 2\) coupling.  This subsection keeps every
factor explicit.  The drive is taken to be resonant, \(\delta(t)=0\).  If
\(\delta\) is not zero, the phase \(e^{\ii\alpha t}\) is replaced by
\(\exp\bigl(\ii\int_0^t(\alpha+\delta(t'))\,\dd t'\bigr)\), while the qubit
propagation is still carried by \(U_q\).

\subsection{Split the Hamiltonian}

Set \(\hbar=1\).  With \(\delta=0\), Eq.~\eqref{eq:h3} splits as
\(H_3=H_q+V\), where the perturbation is only the leakage coupling,
\begin{equation*}
  V(t)=\frac{\lambda}{2}\Bigl(
    \eps(t)\ket1\bra2+\eps^*(t)\ket2\bra1\Bigr),
\end{equation*}
and the ideal part is
\begin{equation*}
  H_q(t)=\alpha\ket2\bra2
  +\frac12\Bigl(\eps(t)\ket0\bra1+\eps^*(t)\ket1\bra0\Bigr).
\end{equation*}
The coefficient \(\lambda/2\) is the coefficient already present in
\eqref{eq:h3}; it is not an extra insertion.  The drive in \(H_q\) acts only
inside \(\{\ket0,\ket1\}\).  Started from a computational state \(\ket j\),
with \(j\in\{0,1\}\), the ideal evolution therefore remains in that subspace.
Amplitude in \(\ket2\) appears only through \(V\).

\subsection{Phase on the leakage transition}

The diagonal term \(\alpha\ket2\bra2\) is a known device energy.  Change
coordinates by the diagonal unitary
\begin{equation*}
  R(t)=\ket0\bra0+\ket1\bra1+e^{-\ii\alpha t}\ket2\bra2,
\end{equation*}
through \(|\psi\rangle=R|\varphi\rangle\).  The term \(-\ii R^\dagger\dot R\)
cancels \(\alpha\ket2\bra2\).  Because \(R\) multiplies only the \(\ket2\)
component, the qubit drive is unchanged, while the leakage operators pick up
phases:
\begin{equation*}
  R^\dagger\ket1\bra2 R=e^{-\ii\alpha t}\ket1\bra2,
  \qquad
  R^\dagger\ket2\bra1 R=e^{\ii\alpha t}\ket2\bra1.
\end{equation*}
The leakage coupling in the new frame is therefore
\begin{equation*}
  \tilde V(t)=\frac{\lambda}{2}\Bigl(
    \eps(t)e^{-\ii\alpha t}\ket1\bra2
    +\eps^*(t)e^{\ii\alpha t}\ket2\bra1\Bigr).
\end{equation*}
The factor \(e^{\ii\alpha t}\) in \eqref{eq:dyson} is this phase on the
\(\ket2\leftarrow\ket1\) term.  Since \(R\) is diagonal and unitary, it changes
the phase of the \(\ket2\) amplitude but not the leakage probability.

\subsection{Interaction picture}

Let \(U_q(t)\) be the evolution generated by the qubit drive in this frame,
embedded in the qutrit space so that it leaves \(\ket2\) unchanged.  Its block
on \(\{\ket0,\ket1\}\) is the ideal gate already computed by SCQC.  The
interaction-picture perturbation is
\begin{equation*}
  V_I(t)=U_q^\dagger(t)\,\tilde V(t)\,U_q(t),
\end{equation*}
and the interaction-picture state obeys
\(\ii\,\dd_t|\varphi_I\rangle=V_I(t)|\varphi_I\rangle\).  The Dyson series of
that equation is
\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
  U_I(T)&=I-\ii\int_0^T V_I(t)\,\dd t\\
  &\quad+(-\ii)^2\int_0^T\dd t_1\int_0^{t_1}\dd t_2\,
    V_I(t_1)V_I(t_2)+\cdots.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation*}
The identity leaves a computational state in the qubit subspace.  The single
integral is the first term that can create \(\ket2\).  The double integral is
second order in \(\lambda\Omega/\alpha\) and is not kept in \eqref{eq:dyson}.

\subsection{Matrix element}

The first-order amplitude to be in \(\ket2\) at time \(T\), starting from
\(\ket j\), is the matrix element of that single integral:
\begin{equation*}
  A^{(1)}_{2j}(T)
  =\braket{2}{U_I(T)j}
  =-\ii\int_0^T\braket{2}{V_I(t)j}\,\dd t.
\end{equation*}
Only the \(\ket2\bra1\) piece of \(\tilde V\) contributes.  The state
\(U_q(t)\ket j\) lies in the qubit subspace, so
\begin{equation*}
  \tilde V(t)U_q(t)\ket j
  =\frac{\lambda}{2}\,\eps^*(t)\,e^{\ii\alpha t}\,
    \ket2\,\braket{1}{U_q(t)j}.
\end{equation*}
The \(\ket1\bra2\) piece would require amplitude in \(\ket2\) already, and
there is none at this order.  Also \(U_q^\dagger\ket2=\ket2\), and therefore
\begin{equation*}
  \braket{2}{V_I(t)j}
  =\frac{\lambda}{2}\,\eps^*(t)\,e^{\ii\alpha t}\,
    \braket{1}{U_q(t)j}.
\end{equation*}
Substitution produces
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:dyson}
  A^{(1)}_{2j}(T)
  =-\ii\frac{\lambda}{2}\int_0^T\dd t\;
    \eps^*(t)\,e^{\ii\alpha t}\,\braket{1}{U_q(t)j}.
\end{equation}
The two factors in the integrand are different objects.
\(\braket{1}{U_q(t)j}\) is the ideal qubit amplitude to occupy \(\ket1\) at
time \(t\); leakage is fed only from \(\ket1\) at this order.  The product
\(\eps^*(t)e^{\ii\alpha t}\) is the drive component seen by the
\(1\leftrightarrow 2\) transition in the frame defined by \(R\).  The complex
conjugate is required because \eqref{eq:h3} couples \(\ket2\leftarrow\ket1\)
with \(\eps^*\), not with \(\eps\).  For one input state, the corresponding
probability is \(\abs{A^{(1)}_{2j}(T)}^2\).

\subsection{Normalized variables}

With \(s=t/T\), \(\dd t=T\,\dd s\), \(u(s)=T\eps(Ts)\), and \(\nu=\alpha T\),
\begin{equation*}
  \eps^*(t)\,\dd t=u^*(s)\,\dd s,
  \qquad
  e^{\ii\alpha t}=e^{\ii\nu s}.
\end{equation*}
The two factors of \(T\) cancel, and \eqref{eq:dyson} becomes
\begin{equation}
  A^{(1)}_{2j}
  =-\ii\frac{\lambda}{2}\int_0^1\dd s\;
    u^*(s)\,e^{\ii\nu s}\,\braket{1}{\tilde U_q(s)j},
\end{equation}
where \(\tilde U_q(s)=U_q(Ts)\).  The moment \(\ell_j(\nu)\) defined below is
this integral without the prefactor \(-\ii\lambda/2\).  Omitting
\(\braket{1}{\tilde U_q(s)j}\) would instead give the Fourier component of the
bare envelope \(u\) at frequency \(\nu\).  That notch condition is weaker than
\(A^{(1)}_{2j}=0\).

Averaging the resulting probability over the two computational inputs gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:leak-prob}
  \bar L^{(1)}
  =\frac12\sum_{j=0,1}\abs{A^{(1)}_{2j}}^2
  =\frac{\lambda^2}{8}\sum_{j=0,1}
    \abs{\ell_j(\nu)}^2,
\end{equation}
where the leakage moments are
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ell}
  \ell_j(\nu)
  \equiv\int_0^1\dd s\;
  u^*(s)\,e^{\ii\nu s}\,\braket{1}{\tilde U_q(s)j}.
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Nominal first-order leakage closure]
\label{prop:leakage}
The first-order leakage amplitude vanishes for every computational input if and
only if
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:leak-conditions}
  \ell_0(\nu)=0,\qquad \ell_1(\nu)=0.
\end{equation}
These are two complex equations, hence four independent real constraints.
\end{proposition}

The cheaper proxy \(\int_0^1 u(s)e^{\ii\nu s}\,\dd s=0\) forces a notch in the
bare envelope spectrum.  It is not equivalent to \eqref{eq:leak-conditions},
because the integrands contain the evolving factor
\(\braket{1}{\tilde U_q(s)j}\).  That factor is available from the ideal qubit
propagator already computed by SCQC, so the stronger condition does not require
a qutrit simulation.

If a physical parameter \(q\) is uncertain, first-order insensitivity of the
leakage cancellation adds
\begin{equation}
  \partial_q\ell_0(\nu)=0,\qquad \partial_q\ell_1(\nu)=0,
\end{equation}
another four real constraints for each such parameter.  The important choices
are anharmonicity, a common amplitude scale, and a static detuning.

\section{DRAG as a post-processor}
\label{sec:drag}

\subsection{Fixed-axis transformation}

Suppose first that the intended envelope is real.  Denote it by \(E_x(t)\),
and write \(E_y(t)\) for a second, corrective quadrature.  These are Motzoi's
\(\mathcal{E}^x\) and \(\mathcal{E}^y\); in the notation of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:h3}, \(E_x=\Omega_x\) and \(E_y=\Omega_y\) before any correction.
The Pauli-like operators that generate the frame change are
\begin{equation}
  Y_{jk}=\ii\ket{k}\bra{j}-\ii\ket{j}\bra{k},
\end{equation}
so \(Y_{01}\) acts on the qubit transition and \(Y_{12}\) on the leakage
transition.  This is Motzoi's \(\sigma^y_{j,k}\).  Following that
paper~\cite{Motzoi2009}, remove the direct leakage coupling by
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:V}
  V(t)=\exp\left\{-\ii\frac{E_x(t)}{2\alpha}
    \bigl(Y_{01}+\lambda Y_{12}\bigr)\right\}.
\end{equation}
The adiabaticity parameter is \(E_x/\alpha\).  To first order in that
parameter, the transformed Hamiltonian contains the inertial term
\begin{equation}
  \frac12\left(E_y+\frac{\dot E_x}{\alpha}\right)(Y_{01}+\lambda Y_{12})
\end{equation}
and an ac-Stark shift of \(\ket1\) proportional to
\begin{equation}
  \delta-\frac{(\lambda^2-4)E_x^2}{4\alpha}.
\end{equation}
Thus the first-order DRAG controls are
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:drag-fixed}
  E_y(t)=-\frac{\dot E_x(t)}{\alpha},
  \qquad
  \delta(t)=\frac{(\lambda^2-4)E_x(t)^2}{4\alpha}.
\end{equation}
For a transmon, \(\lambda^2=2\), so the detuning simplifies to
\(\delta\approx -E_x^2/(2\alpha)\).  Higher-order terms in
Ref.~\cite{Motzoi2009} correct the in-phase envelope, the derivative
quadrature, and the detuning at orders \(E_x^3/\alpha^2\), \(E_x^2\dot
E_x/\alpha^3\), and \(E_x^4/\alpha^3\).  They are useful once \(\eta\) is no
longer small, but they are not required for the first-order theory.

The frame transformation has no net effect on the gate precisely when
\begin{equation}
  V(0)=V(T)=I.
\end{equation}
For \eqref{eq:V}, this is the endpoint condition \(E_x(0)=E_x(T)=0\).

\subsection{Complex envelope}

A general SCQC pulse already uses both quadratures.  The covariant extension of
\eqref{eq:drag-fixed} is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:drag-complex}
  \eps_D(t)=\eps(t)-\ii\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\dot\eps(t),
\end{equation}
with a dimensionless calibration parameter \(\beta\) equal to one in the
analytic first-order theory.  In normalized variables,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:drag-u}
  u_D(s)=u(s)-\ii\frac{\beta}{\nu}u'(s).
\end{equation}
Equivalently, in Cartesian components,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:drag-cartesian}
  \omega_{x,D}=\omega_x+\frac{\beta}{\nu}\omega_y',
  \qquad
  \omega_{y,D}=\omega_y-\frac{\beta}{\nu}\omega_x'.
\end{equation}
If \(u=\omega e^{\ii\phi}\), the same correction is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:drag-polar}
  u_D(s)=e^{\ii\phi(s)}
  \left[\omega(s)+\frac{\beta}{\nu}\omega(s)\phi'(s)
        -\ii\frac{\beta}{\nu}\omega'(s)\right].
\end{equation}
The term proportional to \(\omega\phi'\) is a radial correction generated by
phase modulation.  It is absent from textbook fixed-axis DRAG and should not
be dropped for an SCQC pulse.  Derivatives should be taken on the Cartesian
quadratures: near a signed-envelope zero, \(\omega\) and \(\phi\) separately
can have spurious singularities even though \(\omega_x\) and \(\omega_y\) are
smooth.

\begin{proposition}[Bare spectral notch]
\label{prop:notch}
Suppose \(\eps(0)=\eps(T)=0\).  Then
\begin{equation}
  \int_0^T\left(\eps(t)-\ii\frac{\dot\eps(t)}{\alpha}\right)e^{\ii\alpha t}\,\dd t
  =0.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Integrate the derivative term by parts.  The boundary term is
\((\eps(T)e^{\ii\alpha T}-\eps(0))/\alpha=0\), while
\(-\ii\alpha\int\eps e^{\ii\alpha t}\,\dd t\) cancels the remaining integral of
\(\eps e^{\ii\alpha t}\).
\end{proof}

Proposition~\ref{prop:notch} does not imply
Proposition~\ref{prop:leakage}.  The latter weights \(\eps^*(t)\) by
\(\braket{1}{U_q(t)j}\), and DRAG also changes \(U_q\) at order \(\eta\).
Equation~\eqref{eq:drag-complex} should therefore be read as the appropriate
post-processing ansatz, followed by a small calibration of \(\beta\), an
overall amplitude, and a constant detuning.

\subsection{Boundary conditions and calibration}

The following conditions are listed in the order they become necessary.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \(u(0)=u(1)=0\) makes the first-order DRAG frame an identity at both
        endpoints.  BARQ already encourages this through its vanishing-envelope
        construction, but a loaded or rescaled pulse should be checked.
  \item \(u'(0)=u'(1)=0\) makes the added derivative quadrature start and end
        continuously.
  \item If corrections through the \(m\)th derivative are used, the derivatives
        of orders \(0,\ldots,m\) should vanish at both endpoints.  Otherwise
        integration by parts leaves boundary kicks in the leakage amplitude.
  \item After constructing \(u_D\), at least the global amplitude and a
        constant phase or detuning offset should be recalibrated.  The
        derivative term does not cancel every Stark and higher-order coherent
        error.
\end{enumerate}

A practical post-processing sequence is:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Design \(u(s)\) and \(d(s)\) with ordinary BARQ and record the original
        closure and tantrix-area diagnostics.
  \item Apply \eqref{eq:drag-u} and the Stark correction from
        \eqref{eq:drag-fixed}, transformed into normalized units.
  \item On a qutrit model, tune \(\beta\), the amplitude scale, and a constant
        detuning to minimize \(1-F_{\mathrm{avg}}\) and the leakage \(L\)
        defined in Section~\ref{sec:metrics}.
  \item Recompute noise generators with the corrected propagator, rather than
        reusing the original curve tests.
  \item Repeat under variation of \(\alpha\), gain, timing, sampling, and the
        control-line transfer function.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Robustness after DRAG}
\label{sec:robust}

\subsection{Generator conditions}

The original geometric tests are statements about a qubit propagator and a
qubit noise operator.  Both change once the pulse is corrected and embedded in
a qutrit.  Let \(U_D(t)\) be the ideal qutrit evolution generated by the
corrected controls.  For a parameter \(q_a\) with Hamiltonian derivative
\(V_a(t)=\partial H/\partial q_a\), the first Magnus generator is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:generator}
  G_a=\int_0^T\dd t\; U_D^\dagger(t)\,V_a(t)\,U_D(t).
\end{equation}
Its computational and leakage blocks impose different requirements.

\begin{definition}[First-order qutrit robustness]
The corrected gate is first-order robust against \(q_a\) when
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:robust-blocks}
  PG_aP-\frac12\Tr(PG_aP)\,P=0,
  \qquad QG_aP=0.
\end{equation}
The first equation removes the traceless logical rotation.  The second
prevents the noise from creating leakage to first order.
\end{definition}

For a genuine qubit model one has \(P=I\), and the first equation reduces to
the usual closure condition.  Applying the old closure or tantrix test to the
uncorrected curve is not sufficient, because neither \(U_D\) nor \(V_a\) is the
operator assumed by that test.

\subsection{Perturbation of dephasing closure}

Let \(U_0\) be the computational evolution generated by the uncorrected SCQC
pulse, embedded in the qutrit space.  The corrected propagator can be
factored as
\begin{equation}
  U_D=U_0\exp\bigl[-\ii\eta K_1+\order(\eta^2)\bigr],
\end{equation}
where the Hermitian operator \(K_1(t)\) collects every first-order change
produced by the derivative quadrature and by the frame \(V(t)\).  Its explicit
matrix is not required for the scaling below; only the existence of a bounded
\(K_1\) at order \(\eta\) is used.
For quasistatic detuning noise, take the interaction-picture operator
\(n_0(t)=U_0^\dagger(t)\sigma_z U_0(t)\) and the associated error curve
\(r_0(T)=\int_0^T n_0(t)\,\dd t\).  Expanding \eqref{eq:generator} gives
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:perturbed-curve}
  r_D(T)=r_0(T)+\ii\eta\int_0^T[K_1(t),n_0(t)]\,\dd t+\order(\eta^2).
\end{equation}
Ordinary SCQC can enforce \(r_0(T)=0\).  It does not enforce the commutator
integral.  Consequently, a pulse that is first-order dephasing-robust before
post-processing generically acquires a residual generator of order
\(\eta\).

For a qubit, a vanishing first-order generator changes the leading coherent
infidelity from quadratic to quartic in the noise strength.  After
unconstrained post-processing the scaling is instead
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:scaling-robust}
\begin{aligned}
  \text{original closure:}\quad
    &1-F=\order\bigl((\delta_zT)^4\bigr),\\
  \text{post-processed DRAG:}\quad
    &1-F=\order\bigl((\delta_zT)^2\eta^2\bigr)+\order\bigl((\delta_zT)^4\bigr).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The mixed term dominates the original quartic protection whenever
\(\abs{\delta_zT}\lesssim\eta\).  This is a scaling statement, not a claim that
the numerical coefficient is large.  Restoring the quartic regime requires the
additional vector condition
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mixed}
  \int_0^T[K_1(t),n_0(t)]\,\dd t=0,
\end{equation}
or, more directly, the block conditions \eqref{eq:robust-blocks} evaluated on
the final pulse.

\subsection{Amplitude, DRAG gain, and anharmonicity}

If one calibration error \(a\) multiplies both applied quadratures, the
physical corrected quadratures are \(\Omega_{x,D}(t)=\omega_{x,D}(t/T)/T\) and
\(\Omega_{y,D}(t)=\omega_{y,D}(t/T)/T\).  The Hamiltonian derivative with
respect to that gain is
\begin{equation}
  V_a(t)=\frac12\bigl(\Omega_{x,D}(t)\,\sigma_x+\Omega_{y,D}(t)\,\sigma_y\bigr),
\end{equation}
and robustness is \(G_a\propto I\) on the computational block.  The original
tantrix-area condition is the special case of this statement before DRAG and
before the qutrit embedding.  If the derivative channel is calibrated
independently, \(\beta\) must be treated as its own parameter and \(G_\beta\)
must vanish separately.  Anharmonicity uncertainty is qualitatively different:
it moves the leakage phase \(e^{\ii\nu s}\) and the coefficient \(1/\nu\) in
\eqref{eq:drag-u} simultaneously.  A first-order robust design therefore
penalizes \(\partial_\alpha M\), where \(M=PU_DP\) is the final computational
block.

Second-order cancellation follows from the next Magnus term.  For two error
channels,
\begin{equation}
  G_{ab}^{(2)}=-\frac{\ii}{2}\int_0^T\dd t_1\int_0^{t_1}\dd t_2\;
  \bigl[V_a^I(t_1),V_b^I(t_2)\bigr],
\end{equation}
where the superscript \(I\) denotes the interaction picture generated by
\(U_D\).  The computational block of \(G_{ab}^{(2)}\), with its trace removed,
is the appropriate second-order condition.  In the original qubit dephasing
model this is the geometric zero-area constraint.

\section{Leakage-aware BARQ}
\label{sec:barq}

Post-processing takes a curve designed for a qubit and changes its pulse.
Leakage-aware BARQ instead varies the free B\'ezier points, and optionally a
few gate-fixing parameters, under a qutrit objective.  Three nested models are
useful.

\subsection{Spectral and perturbative objectives}

The least expensive differentiable objective uses
Proposition~\ref{prop:leakage}:
\begin{equation}
  J_{\mathrm{spec}}=\sum_{j=0,1}\abs{\ell_j(\nu)}^2.
\end{equation}
The bare-envelope proxy
\(J_{\mathrm{bare}}=\abs{\int_0^1 u(s)e^{\ii\nu s}\,\dd s}^2\) is cheaper but
weaker.  The corresponding leakage probability from \eqref{eq:leak-prob} is
\begin{equation}
  J_{\mathrm{leak},1}=\frac{\lambda^2}{8}\bigl(\abs{\ell_0}^2+\abs{\ell_1}^2\bigr).
\end{equation}
Both can be evaluated from the normalized envelope and the ideal qubit
propagator.  Their gradients exist once the cumulative phase and arclength are
computed by a differentiable quadrature rather than by a black-box integrator.

\subsection{Exact qutrit objective}
\label{sec:metrics}

Let \(U\) be the full qutrit propagator and \(M=PUP\) its \(2\times2\)
computational block.  For a pure input drawn uniformly from the qubit Bloch
sphere, the average probability of leaving that subspace is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:L}
  L=1-\frac12\Tr(M^\dagger M).
\end{equation}
If \(U_g\) is the target qubit unitary, the Haar-averaged state fidelity is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:F}
  F_{\mathrm{avg}}
  =\frac{\Tr(M^\dagger M)+\abs{\Tr(U_g^\dagger M)}^2}{6}.
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:F} follows from
\begin{equation}
  \int\abs{\bra{\psi}A\ket{\psi}}^2\,\dd\psi
  =\frac{\abs{\Tr A}^2+\Tr(A^\dagger A)}{d(d+1)}
\end{equation}
with \(d=2\) and \(A=U_g^\dagger M\).  The leakage term \eqref{eq:L} is already
present in \(F_{\mathrm{avg}}\), but retaining it separately allows leakage to
be weighted more heavily than a coherent logical rotation.  Both quantities
remain meaningful when \(M\) is not unitary.

\subsection{What remains analytic}

BARQ's point-gate-fixing construction fixes the ideal qubit operation and the
endpoint contribution to dephasing closure before numerical optimization.  On
a qutrit, Stark shifts and virtual transitions modify \(M\), so those
constraints no longer guarantee the physical target exactly.  The useful hybrid
is:

\begin{itemize}
  \item retain point-gate fixing, so the zeroth-order qubit gate and curve
        closure remain exact;
  \item constrain enough endpoint control points that \(u\) and \(u'\) vanish
        at \(s=0,1\);
  \item optimize interior points against \(F_{\mathrm{avg}}\), \(L\), and the
        corrected generators \eqref{eq:robust-blocks};
  \item release a global amplitude and a constant detuning during the final
        stage, since qutrit gate fixing is no longer purely analytic;
  \item test four- and five-level truncations for short gates, where
        two-photon and higher-state transitions are outside \eqref{eq:h3}.
\end{itemize}

A concrete staged objective is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:J}
\begin{aligned}
  J&=w_g(1-F_{\mathrm{avg}})+w_L L
    +w_z\norm{PG_zP-\tfrac12\Tr(PG_zP)P}_F^2
    +w_{zL}\norm{QG_zP}_F^2 \\
   &\quad+w_a\norm{PG_aP-\tfrac12\Tr(PG_aP)P}_F^2
    +w_{aL}\norm{QG_aP}_F^2
    +w_\alpha\norm{\partial_\alpha M}_F^2 \\
   &\quad+w_b\int_0^1\abs{u'(s)}^2\,\dd s
    +w_{bb}\int_0^1\abs{u''(s)}^2\,\dd s
    +w_{\max}\max_s\abs{u(s)} \\
   &\quad+w_{\mathrm{end}}\sum_{k=0,1}\sum_{s_\star\in\{0,1\}}\abs{u^{(k)}(s_\star)}^2.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Every \(w\) is a nonnegative weight chosen by the user, not a physical
constant.  \(G_z\) and \(G_a\) are the generators \eqref{eq:generator} for
\(\delta_z\) and for the common gain \(a\).  The weights should be introduced
in stages: target and original robustness,
then nominal leakage, then parameter uncertainty, and finally bandwidth and
amplitude.  Turning every term on simultaneously makes the trade-off difficult
to diagnose.

\section{Further extensions}

\subsection{Choosing the gate duration}

For a fixed normalized BARQ waveform, the first-order leakage depends on
duration only through \(\nu=\alpha T\).  A coherent-error search can therefore
minimize \(L_1(\nu)=J_{\mathrm{leak},1}\) over \(T\), subject to the hardware
bound on peak amplitude.  This is stronger than placing a zero of the bare
spectrum at \(\alpha\), because \(\ell_j\) contains \(\tilde U_q\).  Decoherence
selects a finite optimum of a model such as
\begin{equation}
  E(T)\approx L_{\mathrm{leak}}(\alpha T)+\frac{c_1T}{T_1}+\frac{c_\phi T}{T_\phi}
  +E_{\mathrm{control}}(T).
\end{equation}
Here \(T_1\) and \(T_\phi\) are the relaxation and pure-dephasing times,
\(c_1\) and \(c_\phi\) are dimensionless averages that depend on the target
gate, \(L_{\mathrm{leak}}\) is the coherent leakage probability, and
\(E_{\mathrm{control}}\) collects coherent control error that remains after
leakage is removed.  The coefficients should come from an open-system
simulation rather than from a universal rule.

\subsection{Endpoint smoothness}

Let \(I(\nu)=\int_0^1 g(s)e^{\ii\nu s}\,\dd s\).  If \(g\) and its first
\(m-1\) derivatives vanish at both endpoints, repeated integration by parts
yields
\begin{equation}
  I(\nu)=\left(-\frac1{\ii\nu}\right)^m\int_0^1 g^{(m)}(s)e^{\ii\nu s}\,\dd s,
\end{equation}
and therefore \(\abs I=\order(\abs\nu^{-m})\).  For leakage,
\(g(s)=u^*(s)\braket{1}{\tilde U_q(s)j}\).  Making only \(u\) smooth does not
automatically make every derivative of this product vanish, but it improves the
decay.  The quadratic penalties on \(u'\) and \(u''\) in \eqref{eq:J} suppress
bandwidth even when an endpoint derivative cannot be constrained exactly.

\subsection{Higher-derivative and multi-transition DRAG}

A linear differential filter can place spectral zeros at unwanted angular
frequencies \(\Delta_k\).  Each \(\Delta_k\) is a transition frequency in
radians per unit time, such as \(\alpha\) or a spectator splitting.  It is not
the control detuning \(\Delta_c\).  Starting from a base dimensionless envelope
\(u_0(t)\),
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:product}
  u_{\mathrm{corr}}(t)
  =\prod_k\left(1-\frac{\ii}{\Delta_k}\frac{\dd}{\dd t}\right)u_0(t).
\end{equation}
Under the Fourier convention \(\int u(t)e^{\ii\omega_F t}\,\dd t\), where
\(\omega_F\) is the Fourier angular frequency and not a Rabi frequency, the
\(k\)th factor contributes \(1-\omega_F/\Delta_k\).  If the filter is instead
applied to the normalized envelope \(u(s)\), replace \(\dd/\dd t\) by
\(T^{-1}\dd/\dd s\) and \(\Delta_k\) by the dimensionless frequency
\(\Delta_k T\).  Expanding
\eqref{eq:product} produces the
higher-derivative DRAG family.  The same construction suppresses several
internal or spectator transitions, provided the corresponding endpoint
derivatives vanish and the coefficients are recalibrated on the full
propagator.  At very short duration, direct and multiphoton transitions beyond
\(\ket2\) can dominate the residual error, so the filter frequencies must
include those transitions or the simulation must retain more levels.

\subsection{Fourier notches and Wah-Wah modulation}

The derivative expansion is not the only way to impose the linear constraints
\(\ell_j(\nu_k)=0\).  Choose real basis functions \(f_n(s)\) on \([0,1]\), for
example a Fourier subset, and write \(u(s)=\sum_n c_n f_n(s)\) with complex
coefficients \(c_n\).  Each notch becomes a linear condition on those
coefficients.  The conditions can be combined with the BARQ gate
area, endpoint, and robustness constraints.  Wah-Wah control is the
crowded-spectrum version of this idea: sideband modulation places a notch near
a neighbour's leakage transition, while a DRAG quadrature suppresses the
addressed qubit's own \(1\leftrightarrow2\)
transition~\cite{Schutjens2013,Vesterinen2014}.  It is relevant when a
spectator transition is closer to the drive than the addressed qubit's
anharmonicity.

\subsection{Relation to multilevel SCQC}

The generalized geometric formalism represents an interaction-picture operator
by coordinates on a generator basis.  Let \(\Lambda_a\), with
\(a=1,\ldots,8\) for a qutrit, be Hermitian generators normalized by
\(\Tr(\Lambda_a\Lambda_b)=2\delta_{ab}\).  These are the Gell-Mann matrices in
the qutrit case and the Pauli matrices when the same formula is reduced to a
qubit.  For an operator \(Q_{\mathrm{B}}\) as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:multi-curve},
\begin{equation}
  q_a(t)=\frac12\Tr\bigl[\Lambda_a U_c^\dagger(t)Q_{\mathrm{B}}U_c(t)\bigr].
\end{equation}
The symbol \(q_a\) is a coordinate, not the uncertain parameter \(q\) used
earlier to denote a generic derivative.  Leading-order cancellation means that
every coordinate closes, \(\int_0^T q_a(t)\,\dd t=0\).  A generic qutrit error
has eight coordinates, so the curve is eight-dimensional.  Generalized
curvatures can be connected recursively to the controls, but a transmon does
not supply enough independent controls to realize an arbitrary curve: two
quadratures and one detuning act with linked \(0\leftrightarrow1\) and
\(1\leftrightarrow2\) matrix elements.

The perturbative intermediate theory avoids that inverse problem.  Let
\(\mathcal{H}\) be the three-dimensional state space.  Leakage amplitudes live
in the four-real-dimensional space \(Q\mathcal{H}P\cong\mathbb{C}^2\),
and the two components of its integrated coordinate are exactly
\((\ell_0,\ell_1)\).  Closure of this leakage curve is
Proposition~\ref{prop:leakage}.  Thus leakage-aware BARQ adds a second,
four-dimensional closure condition without abandoning the existing
\(\mathrm{SU}(2)\) curve used to design the computational gate.

\section{Expected improvement and required evidence}

For a fixed rotation angle, the drive amplitude scales as \(1/T\).  The
dimensionless expansion parameter is therefore \(\eta\sim 1/(\abs\alpha T)\),
up to a pulse-shape coefficient.  A generic off-resonant leakage amplitude
begins at \(\order(\eta)\), so its probability begins at \(\order(\eta^2)\).
First-order DRAG is constructed to cancel that amplitude.  The residual
coherent leakage is then commonly \(\order(\eta^2)\) in amplitude and
\(\order(\eta^4)\) in probability, before decoherence and model error are
added.  Table~\ref{tab:scaling} gives these powers with unit prefactors.  It
is a regime guide, not a predicted infidelity.

Published results show that the attainable improvement depends strongly on
duration and pulse family.  Motzoi~et~al.\ obtain reductions from roughly one
to several orders of magnitude in simulation, depending on the
waveform~\cite{Motzoi2009}.  Early transmon experiments demonstrated
derivative-of-Gaussian DRAG at few-nanosecond
duration~\cite{Chow2010}.  At \(4.16\,\mathrm{ns}\), closed-loop optimization
around DRAG reduced leakage from \(0.29\%\) to \(0.044\%\) and raised the
average Clifford fidelity from \(99.11\%\) to \(99.76\%\) on that
device~\cite{Werninghaus2021}.  Analytical FAST and higher-derivative DRAG
have subsequently reached leakage below \(3\times10^{-5}\) at
\(6.25\,\mathrm{ns}\), about a twentyfold reduction relative to a compared
cosine-DRAG pulse~\cite{Hyyppa2024}.  None of these values can be transferred
directly to a BARQ waveform: its spectrum, phase excursions, anharmonicity, and
coherence environment are different.  Experimental SCQC benchmarking also
identifies leakage as a gate-time-dependent limitation and selects duration by
balancing it against decoherence~\cite{SCQCExperiment2025}.

and coherence times.

\section{Conclusion}

DRAG fits SCQC as a correction in the pulse domain.  For a complex BARQ
envelope the first-order post-processor is \eqref{eq:drag-u}, supplemented by
a quadratic Stark detuning and by the endpoint conditions \(u=u'=0\).  This
exactly notches the bare envelope spectrum, but it only approximately preserves
the computational gate and the original closure condition.  The missing
robustness requirement is \eqref{eq:robust-blocks}, evaluated with the
corrected qutrit evolution; its first correction beyond ordinary curve closure
is the mixed integral \eqref{eq:mixed}.

Leakage-aware BARQ imposes the two complex constraints
\(\ell_0(\alpha T)=\ell_1(\alpha T)=0\) and can include their derivatives with
respect to uncertain parameters.  Exact qutrit propagation remains the
acceptance test, with \(L\) and \(F_{\mathrm{avg}}\) given by \eqref{eq:L}
and~\eqref{eq:F}.  Gate-time selection, endpoint smoothness, higher-derivative
filters, and spectral notches are compatible extensions.  A full
eight-dimensional \(\mathrm{SU}(3)\) construction is consistent with the
general geometric formalism, whereas the practical and geometrically faithful
intermediate step is closure of the perturbative leakage curve in
\(\mathbb{C}^2\).
